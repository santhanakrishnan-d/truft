A custom home is a long chain of short jobs. The worry is rarely the concrete. It is not knowing what is happening, what it will cost, or who is waiting on whom. This page walks that chain, from the block to the keys, the way it usually goes, and the way it goes with Truft.

The life of the house

Where the weeks actually go

The photographs above are finished Truft work. Henson Way is the name of the sample you can click further down, one job shown at lock-up so you can see a week, a cost, and a pause. It is not your house.

Between those photographs, a build passes through you, a designer, a certifier, sometimes council, the builder's office, a site supervisor, each trade, the suppliers, your bank, and the bank's valuer. Each of them does a short piece of real work. The days disappear in the gaps: a note in a pocket, an email under a pile, a Saturday comment that never becomes paper. You are the one paying rent, or interest, while that happens.

Truft can't make weather, a quarry or a supplier who is actually out of stock, council, the certifier, or the bank's valuer any faster. What changes is everything that was waiting only because nobody carried it to the next person with the facts attached.

01 · Choosing

A display home is not your block

You walk a display that faces the right way for photographs. The price on the sheet is one number. Your block slopes to the street and has a tree you may not be allowed to touch. None of that is in the number. The person who poured you water is often not the person who will have the file in month eight.

Elsewhere

You choose a feeling, or a lower number

The allowances are a line you do not yet know how to read. The kitchen in the photograph is not the kitchen in the allowance. By the time the real kitchen exists, the number has grown.

With Truft

You can read a job before you are in one

Open the sample home on this site. We build cost-plus, so the price can move, and every move shows up as its own line with the reason beside it. We will not price your block from a display home. Before you trust any builder's page, this one included, check the builder's licence on the NSW Government register.

02 · The land

The footpath is a poor survey

The block looks manageable from the street. The fall, the easement, the stormwater, the sewer line under the back lawn, the bushfire rating some Hills streets carry, the tree, and the truck that has to deliver a frame will decide more of the budget than the tapware.

Elsewhere

The first plan treats the land as a rectangle

You pay for drawings, then pay again to redraw them. Or you find out when the excavator prices rock, or the driveway no longer fits the crossover. The expensive discovery arrives after you are attached to a plan.

With Truft

A walk, then one page, before drawings get expensive

We walk the block with you. Afterwards you get one page: what we could see, what a survey and a soil test still have to confirm, and what council usually asks of a knockdown on a street like this. It is not an approval and it is not a price. If you build with someone else, the page is still yours.

03 · The price

One number is not a price

A figure on the cover feels like relief. The kitchen, the tiles, the appliances and the driveway are often allowances, and the allowances are for a plainer house than the one in the pictures.

Elsewhere

The small allowance wins the signature

Prime cost items are fittings you choose later, such as taps, tiles and appliances. Provisional sums are work that cannot be priced properly yet, such as rock, stormwater, or asbestos in the old house. Both sit in a schedule near the back of the contract. The real stone and the real rock arrive later, as extras, when changing your mind is expensive.

With Truft

A list you can argue with

Each trade sits at a quoted cost. Anything still a guess is labelled as a guess, with what that guess is expected to buy. Our margin is a line of its own. On the sample home, brass taps were quoted at $2,180 and came in at $2,640 after the owners signed a change of finish. Both numbers are kept. Island stone at $4,200 is not signed, so it has not been spent. Those figures belong to that sample. They are not a quote for your house.

04 · The contract

The evening you sign

The contract is thick because the law is trying to stop the fights people actually have. On the night, most people sign the feeling, not the progress schedule.

Elsewhere

You meet the important clauses afterwards

You find out later what "frame" means to the person who will invoice it, that you were free to choose your own certifier, and that a contract for a new home gives you five clear business days to cool off after you are given a copy. The deposit has often already moved.

With Truft

The same contract, with the traps in ordinary words

It is still a proper NSW home building contract. We'd rather your own lawyer read it before you sign, and anything written in by hand is the first thing to show them. Beside the contract, your page shows the progress schedule in plain language, the allowances, the cooling-off dates, the home building compensation certificate, and a reminder that the certifier is yours to choose. Published NSW guidance caps the deposit at 10 percent. Where that cover is required, the law says you pay nothing, deposit included, until you hold the certificate, and we won't ask you to. A special condition is marked, not buried.

05 · Finishes

The hour in the showroom

You have a gap between school pickup and something else. The stone you put your hand on is beautiful. It is not the stone in the allowance.

Elsewhere

The price arrives after you have half forgotten the other options

Someone mentions the cabinet maker's date in the same breath as the number. You approve tired, or you take the default and live with it. If you wait, the date moves anyway, and you hear about the move from site, not before you decide.

With Truft

Photo, dollars, and days, in one note

Each choice shows the finish, the amount above or below the allowance, and what the dates do if you order this week or next month. Windows often need longer than stone. If your choice pushes a trade who is already booked, you see the new date before you confirm. Nothing is ordered because you liked it out loud. It's ordered once you've signed for it, and a choice that changes the price or the plans becomes a written variation we both sign first.

06 · Approvals

The quiet months

From the street it looks as if nothing is happening. Sometimes that is council. Sometimes the file has not been lodged, and those two silences feel identical from your kitchen table.

Elsewhere

"It's with council" covers three different facts

A question asked three weeks ago and not answered. A design that still does not fit the rules for the lot. A form that is sitting on someone's desk. The excavator was pencilled, then quietly dropped. You hear "these things take time", which is true of council and untrue of an unsent email.

With Truft

You can see who has the file

Some knockdowns in The Hills can use a complying development path. Others need a development application, which council usually shows the neighbours, and once it is approved a construction certificate is still needed before anyone digs. We will not tell you which until the design has been checked against that block. After that, your page shows what was lodged, who has it, what they asked, and the date we are working to. We do not move council's clock. We do not let their clock be the excuse for a question that is actually with us.

07 · On site

The Sunday drive-by

You drive past. The frame is up, or it is not. You text. The reply comes Tuesday, and it does not match what the supervisor said on the footpath.

Elsewhere

Three phones, three versions

Photos arrive when you ask, from whoever still has them. The office has last week's story. You are paying for the place you are living in, and interest on the build, while you try to assemble the truth. Rain is a reason. Silence is not.

With Truft

The day is on the photo

The supervisor's photos land on your page with the day they were taken. The week says the stage, who is booked, and what is next. A booking has a morning. On the sample, waterproofing is Thursday at 10:00, not "sometime". If rain stops a pour, you hear it the day it is known: what slips, and what does not. If a week passes without a photo, you can see the gap. A visible gap is more useful than a cheerful guess.

08 · A change

Said on site, lost by Monday

On a Saturday walk you tell the supervisor you want a stone benchtop instead of the laminate in the allowance, and two more power points. He says he will pass it on. That sentence feels like a decision. In NSW it is not one.

Elsewhere

An hour of work, a couple of weeks of messages

It comes up at the next catch-up. The supplier is emailed. The price comes back. Someone types a variation. It sits under other mail. You ask which colour the price is for, and that answer takes another loop. You sign in the second or third week. The stone needs a couple of weeks of its own. The cabinet maker and the electrician were booked for the old plan, so both move, and the date you told your family slips. The slip started as a memory. A change to the contract or the plans has to be agreed in writing and signed by you and the builder. Until that paper exists, with the price on it, nobody should start the work. If someone starts early, you can be arguing about the bill with the wall already open.

With Truft

The same Saturday becomes paper before it becomes an order

The supervisor puts the request on your page before leaving site, and the office turns it into a question for you: which of these stones, with photos, and a clear line that the price is not in yet. When the supplier answers, you get one page. What changes. How the dollars were worked out. The days if you order now. The days if you wait. We check it and sign it, you sign it, and only then does the order go. Trades who are already booked get the new drawing if their date still holds. If it does not, that fact is on the page before you sign. We will not promise the stone arrives this week. We will not let the delay begin as something someone meant to write down.

09 · The money

The invoice is not the payment

The frame looks finished. You expect a pause, then a bill. What often arrives is a bill, and then your bank tells you something the bill did not.

Elsewhere

Certifier, then you, then the bank, then a valuer

The office books the certifier when someone remembers. The certificate follows the inspection. You forward an invoice. Many lenders want your own contribution paid before they release theirs, and then they send a valuer to look. A complete claim can still take from several days to a couple of weeks, and longer if a certificate is missing or a variation has not been shown to the bank. Nobody put the amount you needed to have ready on a page the week before. You become the person retyping attachments. The builder has already paid the carpenters.

With Truft

You hear the bank's rule before the stage, not during it

Before the stage is due, your page shows the contract amount, what "done" means in your contract, and what your lender has already said they need. We do not guess a bank's rule. We ask you or your broker for it, and we write the answer down, including when your own money has to move first. The certifier is booked against the expected finish, not discovered after. The claim arrives with the photos, and the inspection certificate where the stage has one, in a note a person can forward without translating it. If you want, the same pack goes to your broker. The valuer still comes when the bank sends them. You should not be hearing that name for the first time on the day the money is late.

10 · Quality

The mark you did not want to nag about

A tile that lips, a door that rubs, a mark on stone. You mention it on a walk. Someone says it will all be sorted at the end.

Elsewhere

Handover day is when you try to remember

The removalist is in the drive. Two of the items are now closed in behind joinery. The rest become a tired list, and nobody else was keeping it: the certifier checks the work against the building rules at set stages, not whether the finish is what you paid for. The warranties are real: six years for a major defect and two years for other defects, from completion. They are a slow way to recover something that could have been written down while the trade was still in the room.

With Truft

Now, or at the end, and the page says which

You can add a line yourself, with a photo, or the supervisor does. Paint that can wait is marked as waiting. Anything that will be covered up is marked as now. Each line has a person and a state: open, or done, with a photo of the done. The handover walk is the short list of what is left, not everything you have been storing since lock-up. The warranties still apply. The page does not replace them, and it does not ask you to be the notebook.

11 · The keys

A folder and a phone number

Someone hands you keys, a folder, and a number. The occupation certificate, the variations, the manuals and the waterproofing photos start in different places. Some of them are in an inbox you do not control.

Elsewhere

Three months later you start from the beginning

A tap weeps. You cannot find which model was signed, or whether the leak was already on a list. The supervisor is on the next house. Whoever answers asks you to start from the beginning.

With Truft

The file stays when the supervisor moves on

The contract, the signed changes, the certificates, the manuals you were given, and the lines still open stay on the same page, and at handover you get a full copy to keep, so your records don't depend on us. You write on that thread. The person who replies can see the file. If someone here is on leave, the file does not go on leave with them. After handover we owe you the open lines and the warranties, not a new sales conversation.

What does not change

Honest limits

Weather still stops a slab. A supplier who is actually out of stock still takes their weeks. Council, the certifier, and a bank's valuer still keep their own diaries. A message from you is a request. It becomes a variation only when it is written, priced, and signed by you and by Truft. Until that signature, the work on site does not change and nothing is spent on that change, but rock in the ground or a supplier's price rise can still move the cost, and that arrives as its own line with the invoice and the reason, before it is spent when it can be seen coming. We would rather tell you the limit on the day we know it than sound faster than the job can be.

Plain answers

Questions people are right to ask

So the price can still go up?

Yes. Cost-plus is not a promise that the ground, the finishes and the council will match a guess. It is a promise that you see every invoice and the reason for every move, that our margin stays its own line, and that a change you ask for isn't spent until you and we have both signed a priced variation. When the ground or a supplier moves a cost, you see the new figure and the reason, and if we can see it coming, you see it before it is spent. If a fixed price feels safer, that's a fair choice, and the time to make it is before you sign. We would rather lose the job than explain a surprise in month eight.

What if I am slow to choose a tile?

The date moves on the page before you confirm. A trade who has to be rebooked is a real cost in time. We would rather show you a date we can still keep than hold one we have already lost. Choosing late is allowed. Pretending it was free is not.

My bank is the slow part. What do you actually change?

We put the stage amount, and the rule your lender gave you or your broker, in front of you before the stage finishes. We book the certifier against the expected day, and we send a claim that already has the photos, and the inspection certificate where the stage has one. We do not tell the valuer which day to come. If your own contribution has to be paid first, you will have heard the amount before that week, not from the bank on the day.

We find something wrong after we move in.

The open lines stay on the same page. The statutory warranties still run: six years for a major defect and two years for other defects, from completion. You message that thread. You should not have to retell the job from the slab, and you should not have to find which inbox held the certificate.

NSW Government, contracts for residential building work: published deposit guidance of 10 percent; a variation in writing and signed by both parties; five clear business days to cool off on a contract over $20,000; a home building compensation certificate before payment where that cover is required. Statutory warranties run six years for a major defect and two years for other defects, from completion. Many construction loans use the owner's own contribution before the loan pays, then send a valuer. How long that takes depends on the lender and on whether the pack is complete. The weeks in these scenes are a familiar pattern on a Hills knockdown. They are not a measured average, and they are not a date for your job.
